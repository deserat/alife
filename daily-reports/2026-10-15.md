## What I learned

### The fill is n-dependent even without the boundary

At 23.4/kc g=0: fill = 48.33% (80×80) → 31.58% (160×160) → 24.95% (320×320). The focal bias (movement_bias=0.3, 1-cell steps toward home center) is an absolute spatial scale — on a larger grid, the same 1-cell step covers a smaller fraction of the distance to the home center, making the bias weaker. The fill is n-dependent because the focal bias is less effective at concentrating agents on larger grids.

### The boundary adds <0.5% at g=0.01

At 23.4/kc, fill differences between g=0 and g=0.01: -0.07% (80×80), -0.48% (160×160), +0.05% (320×320). At 46.9/kc: -0.12% (80×80), +0.51% (160×160). The boundary is irrelevant to the fill at this gain — the n-dependence is almost entirely from the focal bias.

### The co-presence is extensive, not intensive — confirmed without the boundary

At 23.4/kc g=0: cp_mean = 0.330 (80×80) → 0.293 (160×160) → 0.452 (320×320). Non-monotonic, with 320×320 highest despite the lowest fill. Same pattern as with the boundary (0.343 → 0.279 → 0.447). The co-presence scales with the absolute material at the boundary between the two halves, which is higher on larger grids (more total cells = more material at the boundary).

### The 53rd mechanism is FALSIFIED

The 53rd mechanism (Session 81) claimed: "the boundary converts density-dependence into n-dependence by acting on the intensive co-presence signal." Three claims, all wrong:
- (a) The co-presence is NOT intensive — it's extensive (Session 83 refined this; Session 84 confirms without the boundary).
- (b) The n-dependence is from the focal bias, not the boundary — the fill is n-dependent even without the boundary.
- (c) The boundary adds <0.5% at g=0.01 — the 50th mechanism's n-dependence (tested at g=0.01-0.02) is from the focal bias.

### The 53rd mechanism's original control was confounded

Session 81 compared mode="none" (no focal bias, no boundary) vs mode="hetero" g=0.01 (focal bias, boundary) — changing two variables simultaneously. The fill was density-dependent in mode="none" and n-dependent in mode="hetero" g=0.01. Session 81 attributed the n-dependence to the boundary. But the correct control is mode="hetero" g=0 (focal bias, no boundary), which shows the fill is n-dependent even without the boundary. The n-dependence is from the focal bias (an absolute-spatial-scale movement mechanism), not from the boundary.

### Methodology lesson: the control must isolate one variable at a time

The 53rd mechanism's control (mode="none") changed two variables (focal bias + boundary) simultaneously, making it impossible to distinguish their effects. The correct control (mode="hetero" g=0) holds the focal bias constant and varies only the boundary. This is the control-arm methodology pattern (#75) recurring: a control that confounds two variables cannot attribute the effect to either.

## Criticisms / limitations (honest)

- **The 50th mechanism's empirical finding holds but its mechanism is wrong.** The 50th mechanism (fill depends on absolute n, not density) is empirically correct at g=0.01 — but the mechanism is the focal bias, not the boundary. At higher gains (g≥0.3), the boundary does contribute to n-dependence, but the 50th mechanism was tested at g=0.01-0.02 where the boundary is negligible.
- **Only 2 seeds for 320×320.** The 320×320 runs took ~125-354s each. The fill and co-presence values are stable across the 2 seeds (both seeds gave similar results).
- **The 320×320 at 46.9/kc (n=4800) was not run** — too expensive for one session (~500-800s per run). The 23.4/kc results are sufficient to falsify the 53rd mechanism.
- **The script's verdict logic is too simplistic.** It checks for monotonic decrease of cp_mean (which would indicate n-dependence), but the cp_mean is non-monotonic (decreases then increases). The correct interpretation is: cp_mean varies across grid sizes (not the same at matched density) → NOT intensive → extensive. The script declared "INTENSIVE" because cp_mean is NOT monotonically decreasing, but the correct interpretation is that the co-presence is extensive (varies with n, just non-monotonically).
